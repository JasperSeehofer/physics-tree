1. **The postulate.** Write the canonical commutation relation for a real scalar field $\varphi$ and its conjugate momentum $\pi$. All of it: which arguments each operator carries, what is on the right-hand side, and any restriction on the arguments. Then write the other two relations of the same set — the ones involving $\varphi$ with $\varphi$, and $\pi$ with $\pi$.

2. **Postulate or result?** Consider $\left[\varphi(x),\varphi(y)\right]$ for two *general* spacetime points $x$ and $y$ — not restricted to the same time. Is that object

   (c) undefined, because commutation relations only make sense at equal times?

## Wonder Hook

On the entry assessment for this programme, asked to canonically quantize the free real scalar, the sheet contains this:

> $[\hat\varphi(x),\hat\varphi(y)]$

with the note, recorded at grading, that the writer "knows the commutator is important". No equal-time relation, no $\pi$, no right-hand side.

That is not a blank and it should not be read as one. It is the *right object written one step early*, and the step in question is the whole of this node.

Why it is the right object: in a relativistic theory it is natural — correct, even — to want the fundamental statements to treat $x$ and $y$ alike, and $[\varphi(x),\varphi(y)] = \text{something}(x-y)$ looks like the covariant statement a covariant theory ought to have. It is also genuinely important: a Lorentz scalar, the same in every frame, vanishing whenever $x$ and $y$ are spacelike separated — the precise sense in which this theory is causal.

Why writing it in *that slot* costs something later: **it is not a postulate, it is a theorem.** Nobody chose its value; it is computed, from the postulate you actually have to make, in about six lines. Its vanishing outside the light cone is a *result*, coming out of a cancellation between the two halves of the mode expansion that fails if either half is dropped. Six nodes from now that cancellation is the argument that antiparticles are the price of causality rather than an optional extra.

So what *is* the postulate? This, with a restriction in it that looks like a retreat and is not:

$$\left[\varphi(t,\mathbf{x}),\,\pi(t,\mathbf{y})\right] = i\,\delta^{3}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}), \qquad \left[\varphi(t,\mathbf{x}),\varphi(t,\mathbf{y})\right] = \left[\pi(t,\mathbf{x}),\pi(t,\mathbf{y})\right] = 0.$$

**Same $t$ on both sides. Every time.** The reason is not that the covariant version is hard or deferred. It is that canonical quantization is not a statement about spacetime at all. It is a statement about a *phase space*: coordinates and conjugate momenta at one instant, with Poisson brackets that Dirac's rule turns into commutators. A phase space lives on a time slice by construction. Asking for the commutator of two fields at different times asks about the *dynamics*, and dynamics is what the Hamiltonian is for. You do not get to postulate it — you compute it, and an attempt to postulate it would almost certainly contradict the equations of motion you also wrote down.

There is a second thing this node does, and it is the more surprising. Node 1 imposed the postulate above and, in the Fourier basis, derived $\left[a_{\mathbf{k}},a^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}'}\right] = (2\pi)^{3}\delta^{3}(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{k}')$. Read casually, that is a *second* fact about the theory. It is not. **The implication runs both ways**: assume only the ladder algebra, take node 1's mode expansions as definitions of $\varphi$ and $\pi$, and the equal-time relation comes back out, deltas, factors and all. Two relations, one content, two bases.

That matters beyond bookkeeping. It tells you what kind of freedom you have when you write a quantum field theory: not "which commutators shall I impose", but "which classical phase space am I quantizing, and does its algebra have a representation of the kind I need". Every later choice in this module — anticommutators for the Dirac field, the constraint problem for Maxwell, the interacting vacuum in LSZ — is a variation on that one question, and the easy case is the only one where you can see the whole argument at once.

By the end of this node you will have written the postulate from memory with its restriction and its two companions, proved the equivalence in both directions, and computed the unequal-time commutator far enough to see what kind of object it is — and to see that node 8 has something real left to do.
